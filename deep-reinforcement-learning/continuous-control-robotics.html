Continuous Control & Robotics (DDPG/SAC/TD3)

A production-and-research treatment organized for engineers who already understand ML, neural networks, and Transformer fundamentals. Each chapter is deliberately split into two reading pages: mechanism first, then systems, failure modes, and research reasoning.

In plain English

Joint torques and steering angles are not a menu you can enumerate and take an argmax over, so continuous-control RL trains an actor network that outputs a real-valued action directly, and the last decade of algorithm design (DDPG → TD3 → SAC) is mostly a story of fixing that actor's instability and overconfidence.

PAGE 1 · MECHANISM, MENTAL MODEL, THEORY

1. Quick explanation

A family of off-policy actor-critic algorithms — DDPG, TD3, and SAC — that extend value-based and policy-gradient RL to continuous action spaces by learning an actor that outputs real-valued actions directly, rather than choosing the best of a finite set.

2. What problem does this solve?

Problem. DQN-style value methods pick an action by computing argmax_a Q(s,a), which is trivial over a small discrete action set. It becomes intractable when actions are continuous vectors — joint torques, steering angle and throttle, gripper force — because there is no way to enumerate or exhaustively search an infinite action space at every timestep. You need a policy that directly outputs a real-valued action and a training signal that tells that policy which direction in action space increases value.

Problem → constraint → design: argmax over a continuous action space is intractable at every control step → the actor's own errors and the critic's Q-value overestimation both compound over long control horizons, so naive actor-critic training is brittle and prone to divergence → deterministic (DDPG) or entropy-regularized stochastic (SAC) actor trained via the policy gradient of a learned critic, with twin critics and target smoothing (TD3) or entropy maximization (SAC) to control overestimation and instability.

Why engineers care: removing the discrete-argmax bottleneck transfers pressure to overestimation bias in the learned critic and brittleness of the deterministic actor to that overestimation. A working continuous-control system therefore instruments Q-value calibration and policy robustness, not just whether the reward curve trends upward.

3. How to think about it

State s (joint angles, velocities, sensor readings)
        │
        ▼
Actor π(s) outputs a continuous action a (deterministic or sampled from a stochastic policy)
        │
        ├── Critic Q(s,a) estimates the value of taking that action
        ├── Twin critics + target smoothing bound overestimation (TD3)
        └── Entropy bonus keeps the policy stochastic and robust (SAC)
        ▼
Actor updated via ∇_a Q(s,a) pushed backward through the actor's parameters

A useful way to think about it: the actor is a function approximator being pushed uphill on a surface (the critic's Q-function) that it did not design and cannot fully trust; DDPG walks straight uphill and falls into every crack the critic's approximation error creates, TD3 refuses to trust a single, possibly-overoptimistic critic and smooths the terrain before stepping, and SAC deliberately keeps some randomness in its gait so it never commits fully to one narrow, possibly-illusory peak.

Where this analogy stops helping: the "terrain" (the critic) is itself being updated online from the actor's own visited states, so the actor and critic are chasing a moving target together — instability is not purely a search problem over a fixed landscape, it is a coupled dynamical system that can diverge if either side moves too fast relative to the other.

4. What is happening under the hood

  1. Represent the state. Continuous state vectors (joint positions/velocities, image or proprioceptive sensors) and continuous action vectors, stored in a replay buffer so off-policy updates can reuse past experience many times.
  2. Execute the topic-specific transformation. DDPG (Lillicrap et al., 2015) extends the deterministic policy gradient theorem: a deterministic actor μ_θ(s) is trained by ascending ∇_θ Q(s, μ_θ(s)), i.e., by backpropagating the critic's gradient with respect to the action straight into the actor's parameters; TD3 (Fujimoto et al., 2018) keeps this deterministic actor but adds twin critics Q1, Q2 and takes the minimum of the two when forming targets (clipped double-Q), delays actor updates relative to critic updates, and adds noise to the target action (target policy smoothing) so the critic cannot be exploited by a sharp, narrow overestimated peak; SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) instead makes the actor stochastic and optimizes the maximum-entropy objective J(π) = E[Σ γ^t (r_t + α·H(π(·|s_t)))], which rewards the policy for staying spread out over good actions rather than collapsing onto a single point estimate.
  3. Apply constraints and allocate resources. Actions are bounded (typically via a tanh squashing on the Gaussian mean in SAC, or explicit clipping in DDPG/TD3) to respect physical actuator limits; exploration in DDPG/TD3 requires externally injected noise (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck or Gaussian) since the actor is deterministic, while SAC gets exploration "for free" from its own entropy-regularized stochastic policy.
  4. Preserve the right state. Target networks for both actor and critic(s), updated via slow Polyak averaging rather than hard copies, keep the bootstrapped TD target from chasing a rapidly moving critic and are as important to stability here as they were in DQN.
  5. Validate the failure boundary. The primary failure pressure is overestimation bias compounding through the deterministic policy gradient: because the actor is trained to directly exploit the critic's estimate, any region where the critic overestimates Q becomes a magnet the actor gets pulled toward, and the resulting policy can look confident while being systematically wrong.
SAC objective: J(π) = E_τ~π[ Σ_t γ^t ( r(s_t,a_t) + α·H(π(·|s_t)) ) ] — deterministic policy gradient: ∇_θ J(μ_θ) = E_s[ ∇_a Q(s,a)|_{a=μ_θ(s)} · ∇_θ μ_θ(s) ]

The resource that usually becomes the problem: critic calibration — how much the learned Q-function overstates the value of actions the actor prefers.

6. How it scales

At a high level, as the workload grows: action-space dimensionality (a 7-DoF arm vs. a 28-DoF humanoid), control frequency, and episode length all increase the number of critic evaluations and the surface area for overestimation to compound. The practical bottleneck is critic overestimation propagating through the actor update, which may dominate before wall-clock training compute does.

T_convergence = T_exploration + T_critic_calibration + T_actor_stability + T_sim_to_real_validation

The practical question is: at what action-space dimensionality or control frequency does critic overestimation bias become the first thing that destabilizes training, and does the chosen algorithm's fix (TD3's clipped double-Q, SAC's entropy regularization) actually address that specific failure mode at your scale?

7. How it works in production

OFFLINE / ASYNC

    ↓
Store in replay buffer; version data

Train actor + twin critics off-policy
    ↓
Evaluate on held-out seeds/tasks; promote

ONLINE / CRITICAL PATH

Observation (joint state, sensors)
    ↓
Actor forward pass → continuous action
    ↓
Action bounds / safety clipping
    ↓
Actuate; log transition to replay buffer
    ↓
Monitor Q-value calibration + action saturation

A practical production starting point: start with SAC as the default off-policy continuous-control algorithm. Its automatic temperature tuning and inherent stochastic exploration make it noticeably less hyperparameter-brittle than DDPG in practice — which is why Haarnoja et al.'s follow-up work ("Soft Actor-Critic Algorithms and Applications," 2018) reports training real quadruped and manipulator hardware directly, with only a few hours of real-world rollout time. SAC's stability tolerates the noisier, less-resettable nature of physical rollouts better than DDPG.

  • Keep replay buffers versioned and separable by data source (simulation vs. real rollouts) so a distribution shift between them is visible.
  • Make actor and critic checkpoints independently rollback-able.
  • Bound and log action saturation (how often the actor is pinned at its output limits) as an early signal that the policy is fighting the action-bound constraint rather than solving the task.

Use DDPG when: you need the simplest deterministic-actor baseline for a well-understood, low-dimensional control task and are prepared to tune exploration noise carefully. Use TD3 when: DDPG-style training is unstable and you want the same deterministic-actor simplicity with materially better overestimation control. Use SAC when: you want the most robust default for real robotics work, especially when real-world rollouts are expensive and you cannot afford many failed training runs to hand-tune exploration. Avoid all three without a critic-calibration check when: you cannot yet measure whether the learned Q-values track empirical returns.

10. Common misunderstandings

Misconception: "SAC's entropy term is just an exploration bonus, similar to epsilon-greedy."
Reality: the entropy term is part of the optimized objective itself (maximum-entropy RL), not an exploration heuristic bolted on afterward — the resulting policy is trained to be robust to perturbation, not merely to explore more during training.
Why people believe it: the practical effect (more varied actions during training) looks similar to classic exploration bonuses, which obscures that the entropy term changes what "optimal" means.

Misconception: "TD3 and SAC are strictly better than DDPG in every situation, so there's no reason to understand DDPG."
Reality: DDPG's deterministic policy gradient is the mechanism both later algorithms build on, and it can still be the right, simpler choice for a small, well-behaved control task where TD3/SAC's extra machinery is unnecessary overhead.
Why people believe it: benchmark tables in the TD3 and SAC papers report DDPG as the weaker baseline, which is true on the specific tasks measured but does not mean DDPG is universally obsolete.

Misconception: "A policy that achieves high return in a MuJoCo/simulated benchmark is ready for real hardware."
Reality: continuous-control algorithms are typically validated in simulation, and the actor's confidence in a learned critic's Q-values does not transfer automatically to real dynamics, sensor noise, or actuator limits.
Why people believe it: simulated benchmark leaderboards are the dominant way these algorithms are compared in the literature, which understates the additional sim-to-real work required before deployment.

11. The point where this starts to hurt

The system appears to be learning well because the training return climbs, but the real bottleneck is whether the critic's Q-value estimates are calibrated against actual returns. Capacity and safety planning must model critic overestimation and action saturation as they scale with action-space dimensionality and control frequency — not just the headline return curve.

12. How to explain it in an interview (30–45 seconds)

"DDPG, TD3, and SAC extend actor-critic RL to continuous action spaces, where you can't do argmax over actions the way DQN does. DDPG trains a deterministic actor directly against the gradient of a learned critic, using the deterministic policy gradient theorem, but that makes it very sensitive to the critic's overestimation bias. TD3 fixes this with clipped double-Q learning, delayed actor updates, and target policy smoothing. SAC instead makes the actor stochastic and adds an entropy bonus to the objective, which gives it built-in, more robust exploration and generally more stable training. The main trade-off is stability and robustness versus simplicity — SAC is usually the safer default, especially for real robotics, because real-world rollouts are expensive and you can't afford many failed runs. In production I'd track whether the critic's Q-values are actually calibrated against empirical returns, not just whether the reward curve is going up, and I'd treat sim-trained policies as unvalidated until tested against real-hardware dynamics."

13. Follow-up questions you may get

  1. Why can't you just discretize the continuous action space and use DQN? Short answer: discretization either loses precision (coarse bins) or blows up combinatorially with dimensionality (fine bins across many joints), which is exactly the problem a direct continuous-action actor avoids.
  2. What specifically does TD3 change relative to DDPG? Deep explanation: clipped double-Q (min of two independently trained critics) to reduce overestimation, delayed actor updates so the actor doesn't chase a rapidly-changing critic, and target policy smoothing (noise added to the target action) to prevent the critic from being exploited by a sharp, narrow peak.
  3. Why does SAC tend to be more sample-efficient in practice? Trade-off: its entropy-regularized objective keeps the policy exploring useful alternative actions throughout training rather than prematurely collapsing to a single deterministic behavior, at the cost of a slightly more complex objective and temperature parameter to tune (or auto-tune).
  4. What is the simplest production baseline? Start with SAC and its default automatic temperature tuning, validate critic calibration against empirical returns, and only move to TD3/DDPG if you have a specific reason (e.g., needing a fully deterministic policy for reproducibility or certification).
  5. What alternative would you choose for a real robot with limited real-world trials? Compare model-based RL or offline RL against these on-policy-adjacent off-policy methods when real rollouts are extremely expensive — sometimes the right answer is to learn a dynamics model first and plan, rather than model-free actor-critic training from scratch.

That graph is more useful than memorizing isolated definitions.

15. What to remember

The Core Mental Model

Continuous Control & Robotics is best understood as training an actor to directly climb the gradient of a learned critic in a continuous action space, where DDPG, TD3, and SAC differ mainly in how aggressively they protect the actor from the critic's own estimation errors.

The Production Insight

At scale, the headline training return is only one component of the system. Critic calibration, action saturation, and the gap between simulated and real dynamics determine whether the theoretical algorithmic benefit survives contact with real hardware.

The Staff+/Research Insight

The deeper principle is that off-policy actor-critic training is a coupled dynamical system, not a search over a fixed landscape — the actor is only as good as the critic it is trained against, and every meaningful advance in this family (TD3, SAC) is really an advance in keeping that coupling stable rather than a new way of representing the policy itself.

Production Case Study — Soft Actor-Critic training real robotic hardware

System: Soft Actor-Critic on real quadruped and manipulator hardwareEvidence: public paper (Haarnoja et al., "Soft Actor-Critic Algorithms and Applications," 2018)Scope: off-policy continuous control, real-world sample efficiency

Binding constraint

Real robot rollouts are slow, cannot be trivially reset or parallelized like a simulator, and any training instability risks damaging physical hardware. The algorithm therefore has to be both sample-efficient and robust to hyperparameter choices from the very first real trial.

Design intervention

SAC's entropy-regularized objective and automatic temperature tuning removed the need for a hand-tuned exploration-noise schedule. Its off-policy replay-buffer reuse let a handful of hours of real-world experience train working locomotion and manipulation policies directly on hardware, without a simulator in the loop.

expensive, non-resettable real rollouts → need stability + sample efficiency without hand-tuning → maximum-entropy actor-critic objective (SAC) → automatic temperature tuning removes exploration-schedule fragility → real hardware trained in hours, not days

2. AI Researcher test

Separate mechanism from evidence. For every important claim, ask whether it is a proven result, an empirical finding, or an engineering heuristic. The critical test for this topic is whether a reported sample-efficiency or stability gain survives a change of benchmark task, action-space dimensionality, or reward scale.

  • What baseline (DDPG) makes the TD3/SAC comparison meaningful?
  • Which assumption (accurate critic, well-tuned entropy temperature) would invalidate the result?

3. Staff AI Engineer test

Can the technique survive production load and organizational complexity? Focus on critic calibration monitoring and real-hardware safety validation. A design is incomplete until ownership, degradation behavior, rollback, and observability are specified.

  • Where is the stateful failure boundary — replay buffer composition (sim vs. real data)?
  • What degrades first under a higher-dimensional action space or higher control frequency?
  • What can be changed independently (critic architecture, entropy temperature) without invalidating existing checkpoints?
