Policy Gradient Methods & Actor-Critic

A production-and-research treatment organized for engineers who already understand ML, neural networks, and Transformer fundamentals. Each chapter is deliberately split into two reading pages: mechanism first, then systems, failure modes, and research reasoning.

In plain English

Instead of learning action values and picking the best one, policy gradient methods directly adjust the probability of every action a network took, in proportion to how much better or worse than expected the outcome turned out to be — and actor-critic architectures add a learned "expectation" model so that judgment doesn't have to wait until the end of an episode.

PAGE 1 · MECHANISM, MENTAL MODEL, THEORY

1. Quick explanation

Policy gradient methods directly parameterize a stochastic policy π_θ(a|s) and ascend an estimate of the expected-return gradient given by the policy gradient theorem, rather than deriving actions indirectly from a learned value function; actor-critic architectures pair this policy ("actor") with a learned value function ("critic") that supplies a lower-variance, bootstrapped estimate of how good an action was.

2. What problem does this solve?

Problem. Value-based methods like DQN (previous chapter) require an argmax over actions both to act and to compute training targets. That breaks down for continuous action spaces (joint torques, steering angles) and cannot represent a genuinely stochastic policy, which some environments (partial observability, competitive games) actually require for optimal behavior.

Problem → constraint → design: need to optimize a policy directly, including continuous/stochastic action spaces where argmax is unavailable or undesirable → naive Monte Carlo policy-gradient estimates (REINFORCE) have very high variance, since one noisy trajectory return credits every action equally → subtract a learned state-value baseline (advantage), and use a bootstrapped critic (actor-critic) or Generalized Advantage Estimation to trade bias for variance.

Why engineers care: removing the argmax-over-actions constraint transfers pressure to the variance of the policy-gradient estimator and strict on-policy data requirements. A useful implementation therefore instruments gradient variance and effective sample size rather than only watching the return curve, since a noisy but unbiased gradient can look identical to a biased one in a short training run.

A useful way to think about it: REINFORCE says "if this trajectory did better than expected, increase the probability of every action in it, in proportion to the surprise (the advantage)" — it is credit assignment by correlation, not causation, over an entire episode.

Where this analogy stops helping: unlike ordinary supervised gradient descent, the policy being updated is also the process generating the very data the gradient is computed from — a large or biased update doesn't just degrade a metric, it can change what data the agent collects next, potentially in a self-reinforcing way that DQN's off-policy replay buffer is structurally immune to.

4. What is happening under the hood

  1. Represent the state. A policy network π_θ(a|s) (categorical/softmax head for discrete actions, Gaussian head for continuous actions) and, in actor-critic variants, a separate or shared value network V_φ(s) acting as the critic.
  2. Execute the topic-specific transformation. Collect trajectories under the current policy, compute the advantage estimate Â_t (Monte Carlo return minus baseline, n-step bootstrapped, or GAE), then ascend ∇_θ J ≈ E[∇_θ log π_θ(a|s) · Â].
  3. Apply constraints and allocate resources. Because the gradient estimator's expectation is only valid under the current policy's own state-action distribution, data collected under an older policy cannot be reused for more than a very limited number of updates — this on-policy requirement is the direct trade for supporting continuous/stochastic actions.
  4. Preserve the right state. The critic's parameters φ persist and are updated via ordinary TD regression toward observed returns; the actor's parameters θ are updated using the critic's advantage estimate, making the two networks' training dynamics coupled.
  5. Validate the failure boundary. The primary failure pressure is gradient-estimate variance: a single noisy or unrepresentative batch of trajectories can produce an update that meaningfully degrades the policy, and because the policy also generates future training data, that degradation does not automatically self-correct.
Policy gradient theorem: ∇_θ J(θ) = E_{s~d^π, a~π_θ}[∇_θ log π_θ(a|s) · Q^π(s,a)] — any state-dependent baseline b(s) subtracted from Q^π(s,a) leaves this expectation unbiased while reducing its variance. GAE: Â_t^{GAE(γ,λ)} = Σ_l (γλ)^l δ_{t+l}, where δ_t = r_t + γV(s_{t+1}) − V(s_t)

The resource that usually becomes the problem: gradient-estimate variance and the strictly on-policy data requirement, which together bound sample efficiency far more than raw compute does.

PAGE 2 · PRODUCTION, TRADE-OFFS, INTERVIEW REASONING

6. How it scales

At a high level, as the workload grows: as episode horizon T grows, the variance of a Monte Carlo return estimator grows with it, and credit assignment across a long trajectory becomes noisier — a reward received at step 200 gets (correctly, but noisily) attributed back through 200 multiplied log-probabilities. The practical bottleneck is gradient-estimate variance, which may dominate before raw compute does; parallel rollout workers (A3C/A2C-style) amortize wall-clock cost by increasing effective batch size, not by reducing per-sample variance directly.

T_request = T_queue + T_compute + T_memory + T_network + T_validation

The practical question is: at what horizon length or reward sparsity does gradient variance dominate the learning signal to the point that more parallel rollouts, not more gradient steps, is the correct lever? That threshold is more actionable than a single big-O label.

A practical production starting point: follow four practices together.

  • Run multiple vectorized environment copies (e.g. Gymnasium's `VectorEnv`) to collect large, low-variance on-policy batches per update.
  • Normalize advantages per batch.
  • Anneal an entropy-bonus coefficient over training to prevent premature convergence to a deterministic policy.
  • Clip gradients to bound the size of any single update.

Keep durable state behind explicit ownership boundaries; make workers independently replaceable; version caches and artifacts; isolate slow or failure-prone dependencies behind queues, deadlines, and budgets.

Use the simpler approach when: you need an unbiased correctness reference and episodes are short enough that Monte Carlo variance is tolerable. Use actor-critic when: episodes are long or reward is sparse enough that waiting for full-episode returns makes learning too slow. Choose GAE with a tuned λ when: you've measured that the bias/variance trade-off at either extreme (pure Monte Carlo or pure one-step TD) is the limiting factor. Avoid both raw REINFORCE and an over-tuned GAE sweep when: you have not yet confirmed whether variance, bias, or on-policy sample cost is the actual bottleneck for your environment.

10. Common misunderstandings

Misconception: "Subtracting a baseline changes what the policy gradient is optimizing."
Reality: any state-dependent (not action-dependent) baseline leaves the expected value of the policy gradient exactly unchanged — E[∇log π(a|s)·b(s)] = 0 — so a baseline only reduces variance, it never introduces bias, which is precisely why the critic is safe to add.
Why people believe it: it looks like you're "changing the reward," which sounds like it should change the objective, when in fact the mathematics of the expectation cancels the baseline term exactly.

Misconception: "Policy gradient methods are strictly better than value-based methods because they're 'more direct.'"
Reality: policy gradient methods trade away DQN's off-policy sample efficiency (reusing old data via replay) for the ability to represent continuous and stochastic action distributions — it's a trade-off, not a strict improvement.
Why people believe it: policy gradients are usually introduced as "the more general" formulation, which is true in expressiveness but not in sample efficiency.

Misconception: "A prototype architecture is a production architecture."
Reality: production requires versioning, observability, isolation, rollback, evaluation, and explicit state ownership.
Why people believe it: prototypes optimize for demonstrating a mechanism, not surviving failure.

11. The point where this starts to hurt

The system appears to be improving because the average return trends upward, but the real bottleneck is that a single high-variance or oversized update can collapse the policy, and the policy is also the mechanism generating the data used to detect that collapse. Capacity planning must therefore track per-update policy-behavior change (e.g. approximate KL divergence between successive policies), not only the smoothed return curve, which can mask a recent destructive update for several iterations.

12. How to explain it in an interview (30–45 seconds)

"Policy gradient methods directly optimize a parameterized policy by ascending an estimate of the policy gradient theorem's expectation, instead of deriving actions from a learned action-value function. They exist because value-based methods need an argmax over actions, which breaks down for continuous or genuinely stochastic action spaces. Operationally, the pipeline is: roll out trajectories under the current policy, compute an advantage estimate — ideally with a learned critic baseline and GAE rather than raw Monte Carlo returns — and ascend the log-probability-weighted advantage. I think of REINFORCE as credit assignment by correlation: increase the probability of everything in a trajectory that did better than expected. The main trade-off is that policy gradients are strictly on-policy, so you lose DQN's replay-buffer sample efficiency in exchange for continuous-action support, and the gradient estimator's variance can be high enough that a single bad batch meaningfully damages the policy. In production I'd monitor per-update KL divergence between successive policies as an early-warning signal, not just the smoothed return curve."

13. Follow-up questions you may get

  1. What is the dominant resource at scale? Short answer: gradient-estimate variance and the strictly on-policy data requirement — both limit sample efficiency more than raw compute.
  2. What assumption makes the mechanism work? That a state-dependent baseline leaves the policy gradient's expectation unbiased while reducing its variance — this is what makes the critic safe to introduce.
  3. Where does it fail first? When a single oversized update meaningfully shifts the action distribution, since the actor is also the data-generating process and there is no off-policy replay buffer to fall back on.
  4. What is the simplest production baseline? An actor-critic with GAE, per-batch advantage normalization, entropy regularization, and gradient clipping, evaluated by tracking per-update KL divergence alongside return.
  5. What alternative would you choose? If destructive updates are the measured failure mode, move to trust-region or proximal methods (next chapter), which explicitly bound the per-update change in policy behavior.

15. What to remember

The Core Mental Model

Policy Gradient Methods & Actor-Critic is best understood as directly ascending the expected return by reinforcing actions in proportion to how much better than expected they turned out to be, where "expected" comes from either a Monte Carlo return or, more efficiently, a learned critic.

The Production Insight

At scale, the main mechanism is only one component of the system. Gradient-estimate variance, the on-policy data constraint, and the risk of a single destructive update determine whether the theoretical elegance of policy ascent survives contact with a long-running, real training loop.

The Staff+/Research Insight

The deeper principle connecting this chapter to the next is that "reduce variance without adding bias" (the baseline/critic) and "bound how far a single update can move the policy" (the next chapter's trust region) are two independent axes of the same underlying problem — an update that is too noisy or too large is dangerous for the same structural reason: the policy is both the object being optimized and the process generating the data used to optimize it.

Research, evidence, and what it means in practice

This pass replaces generic literature guidance with topic-specific research anchors. Each source is separated into what it demonstrated and how the idea is commonly operationalized today.

1. Simple statistical gradient-following algorithms for connectionist reinforcement learning (REINFORCE)
Williams · 1992

What it demonstratedDerived that the gradient of expected return with respect to policy parameters can be estimated from sampled trajectories using the log-derivative trick, without differentiating through the environment's dynamics.

How practitioners use itREINFORCE's log-probability-weighted-return update is the mathematical core that every later actor-critic and trust-region method still builds on.

2. Policy Gradient Methods for Reinforcement Learning with Function Approximation
Sutton, McAllester, Singh & Mansour · NeurIPS 2000

What it demonstratedProved the policy gradient theorem in its modern form and showed that a compatible function approximator for the value function can be substituted into the gradient without introducing bias.

How practitioners use itThis is the theoretical justification for why an actor-critic architecture — a separately parameterized critic — is a mathematically sound design, not just a practical convenience.

3. High-Dimensional Continuous Control Using Generalized Advantage Estimation
Schulman et al. · 2016

What it demonstratedIntroduced a single λ parameter that interpolates between low-bias/high-variance Monte Carlo advantage estimates and low-variance/high-bias one-step TD advantage estimates.

How practitioners use itGAE(γ,λ) is now the default advantage estimator underlying nearly every modern actor-critic and proximal policy method, including the trust-region methods in the next chapter.

Production Case Study — Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C)

System: A3C (DeepMind, ICML 2016)Evidence: public paper / documented systemScope: parallel on-policy training infrastructure

Binding constraint

Actor-critic methods need fresh, on-policy, decorrelated data. Unlike DQN, they cannot rely on a large replay buffer of stale transitions to decorrelate samples — reusing old off-policy data would bias the policy gradient.

Design intervention

Run many CPU-only environment instances and actor-critic workers asynchronously in parallel, each computing gradients against its own local rollout and periodically applying updates to a shared global network. This decorrelates samples through parallel diversity of experience instead of through a stored buffer.

on-policy data requirement forbids a stale replay buffer → single-worker data is correlated and slow to collect → many asynchronous parallel actor-critic workers → decorrelated, high-throughput on-policy training without GPU-heavy infrastructure
SymptomRoot mechanismWhat to measure
Capacity, latency, quality, or reliability degrades under scale.On-policy data requirement forbids the replay-buffer decorrelation strategy value-based methods use.Per-update KL divergence between policy snapshots, wall-clock throughput across parallel workers, and variance of the advantage estimator.
Generalizable engineering lesson: When an algorithm cannot buffer and replay stale data for structural reasons (on-policy correctness), the systems answer is usually to decorrelate through parallelism instead of storage.
