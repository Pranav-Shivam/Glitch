MDPs & RL Foundations

A production-and-research treatment organized for engineers who already understand ML, neural networks, and Transformer fundamentals. Each chapter is deliberately split into two reading pages: mechanism first, then systems, failure modes, and research reasoning.

In plain English

A Markov Decision Process is the formal contract that says "here is a state, here are the actions you can take, here is how the world reacts, here is the reward" — and the Bellman equation is the recursive rule that turns "what should I do now" into a computable, checkable quantity instead of a guess.

PAGE 1 · MECHANISM, MENTAL MODEL, THEORY

1. Quick explanation

An MDP formalizes sequential decision-making under uncertainty as a tuple (S, A, P, R, γ) — states, actions, a transition distribution, a reward function, and a discount factor — and the Bellman equation gives the recursive structure that lets value functions and optimal policies be computed or learned rather than searched for exhaustively.

2. What problem does this solve?

Problem. Before you can write "an algorithm that makes good decisions," you need a precise definition of what a decision, a consequence, and "good" even mean when actions affect a future that unfolds stochastically over many steps. Otherwise there is no way to define optimality, no way to prove an algorithm converges, and no shared vocabulary between a tabular grid-world and a robot arm.

Problem → constraint → design: undefined notion of sequential optimality under uncertainty → intractable exhaustive lookahead over all future trajectories → define state/action/reward/transition/discount factor, define value functions, derive the Bellman equation as a recursive contraction solvable by dynamic programming.

Why engineers care: removing the "how do we even define this" bottleneck transfers pressure to the curse of dimensionality and the requirement for a known model. A useful implementation therefore instruments which resource — state enumeration, model knowledge, or sample count — actually limits the method, rather than treating "it's an MDP" as if that alone guarantees tractability.

3. How to think about it

Current state s_t
        │
        ▼
Agent picks action a_t ~ π(·|s_t)
        │
        ▼
Environment: s_{t+1} ~ P(·|s_t,a_t), reward r_t = R(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1})
        │
        ├── Bellman backup: V(s) ← Σ_a π(a|s) Σ_s' P(s'|s,a)[R + γV(s')]
        ├── discount factor bounds the effective horizon
        └── Markov property: s_t alone must be sufficient to predict s_{t+1}
        ▼
Updated value estimate / improved policy

A useful way to think about it: an MDP is a stochastic shortest-path graph where edge weights are random rewards instead of fixed costs. The Bellman equation applies the same recursive relaxation idea as Bellman-Ford or Dijkstra — the value of a node is the best immediate step plus the (discounted) value of wherever you land next.

Where this analogy stops helping: a shortest-path graph is fully known and static; an MDP's transition kernel is usually stochastic, the horizon can be effectively infinite, and in almost every real problem you do not know P or R in closed form — you only get to sample them by acting, which is precisely what motivates learning-based methods (Q-learning, policy gradients) rather than pure dynamic programming.

4. What is happening under the hood

  1. Represent the state. Choose a state representation that satisfies the Markov property: P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t) must not depend on s_0...s_{t-1}. If it does not — e.g. a single camera frame in a game with hidden velocity information — the problem is really a Partially Observable MDP (POMDP), and value estimates computed as if it were Markov will be systematically wrong.
  2. Execute the topic-specific transformation. Apply the Bellman backup: V(s) ← max_a Σ_s' P(s'|s,a)[R(s,a,s') + γV(s')] for value iteration, or alternate full policy evaluation with greedy policy improvement for policy iteration.
  3. Apply constraints and allocate resources. The discount factor γ ∈ [0,1) trades off effective planning horizon against convergence speed — both value iteration and policy evaluation are contraction mappings under the sup-norm precisely because γ < 1, which is what guarantees convergence to a unique fixed point.
  4. Preserve the right state. Decide whether you store V(s) (requires knowing P and R at decision time to act, since you must simulate one step ahead) or Q(s,a) (lets you act by simply taking argmax_a Q(s,a), with no model needed at decision time) — this single representational choice is the fork in the road that separates classical dynamic programming from the model-free methods in the next chapter.
  5. Validate the failure boundary. The primary failure pressure is the curse of dimensionality: tabular dynamic programming requires |S| and |A| to be small enough to enumerate and store, and a per-iteration cost of roughly O(|S|²|A|), which fails immediately for continuous or high-dimensional state spaces such as raw pixels or joint-torque vectors.
Bellman optimality equation: V*(s) = max_a Σ_s' P(s'|s,a)[R(s,a,s') + γV*(s')], and Q*(s,a) = Σ_s' P(s'|s,a)[R(s,a,s') + γ max_a' Q*(s',a')]

The resource that usually becomes the problem: the curse of dimensionality and the requirement for a known transition model — real environments rarely hand you P(s'|s,a) in closed form.

6. How it scales

At a high level, as the workload grows: as |S| and |A| grow — more grid cells, more joints, richer sensors — tabular value iteration's per-sweep cost and memory grow at least quadratically in |S|, and a continuous state space makes exact tabular representation impossible outright. The practical bottleneck is the curse of dimensionality, which may dominate long before anyone runs out of raw FLOPs.

T_request = T_queue + T_compute + T_memory + T_network + T_validation

The practical question is: at what state-space size does exact dynamic programming stop being feasible, forcing a switch to sampling-based, model-free, or function-approximation methods? That threshold — not the abstract "MDPs are solvable" claim — is what determines whether Chapter 91's formalism can be solved directly or only approximated by the methods in the chapters that follow it.

A practical production starting point: express the environment through a standardized interface — the OpenAI Gym / Gymnasium `env.reset()` / `env.step(action)` contract is the de facto convention — so that the state, action space, and reward function are explicit, versioned, and swappable independently of whichever algorithm consumes them.

Use the simpler approach when: the state and action spaces are small enough to enumerate and you have (or can estimate) the transition model — value iteration then gives an exact answer with no learning-stability concerns. Use policy iteration when: policy evaluation converges quickly relative to the cost of a full sweep and you want fewer outer iterations. Choose learned/sample-based methods when: the state space is too large or continuous to enumerate, or the transition model is unknown and must be inferred from interaction. Avoid both naive tabular DP and premature function approximation when: you have not yet confirmed whether your state representation is actually Markov — fixing that first prevents debugging a convergence problem that is actually a representation problem.

10. Common misunderstandings

Misconception: "The state is just whatever sensor data or features happen to be available."
Reality: a valid MDP state must satisfy the Markov property — if the optimal action at a given observation depends on history the observation doesn't encode, value functions computed over it are not well-defined, only approximately so.
Why people believe it: in practice engineers often reuse whatever features a supervised pipeline already produces, without checking whether they discard information the policy actually needs.

Misconception: "Formulating a problem as an MDP means you need to know the transition probabilities."
Reality: the MDP is a mathematical formalism describing the problem, not a requirement on the solution method — dynamic programming (this chapter) needs a known model, but model-free reinforcement learning (Q-learning, policy gradients, covered in the next chapters) only ever needs samples from the same underlying MDP, never P(s'|s,a) in closed form.
Why people believe it: introductory treatments often present value iteration first, which does require the model, and the model-free chapters look deceptively unrelated at first glance.

Misconception: "A prototype architecture is a production architecture."
Reality: production requires versioning, observability, isolation, rollback, evaluation, and explicit state ownership.
Why people believe it: prototypes optimize for demonstrating a mechanism, not surviving failure.

11. The point where this starts to hurt

The system appears theoretically sound because the Bellman equation is exact, but the real bottleneck is the curse of dimensionality and the model-knowledge requirement. Capacity planning for any RL system must therefore start by asking whether the state space is small enough for dynamic programming at all, and whether a model of P and R is actually available — not by assuming the elegance of the formalism transfers automatically to a tractable algorithm.

12. How to explain it in an interview (30–45 seconds)

"An MDP is the tuple (S, A, P, R, γ) that formalizes sequential decision-making under uncertainty, and the Bellman equation gives the recursive relationship that value functions must satisfy. It exists because without this formalism there's no rigorous definition of an optimal policy or a way to prove an algorithm converges. Operationally, dynamic programming solves it by repeatedly applying the Bellman backup — value iteration or policy iteration — until the value function is a fixed point. I think of it as a stochastic shortest-path problem where the Bellman equation is the recursive relaxation step. The main trade-off is that exact dynamic programming needs a known model and an enumerable state space; real problems rarely have both, which is exactly why model-free RL and function approximation exist. In production I'd first validate that the chosen state representation is actually Markov, because a policy trained on a non-Markov state can look optimal in evaluation and fail unpredictably in deployment."

13. Follow-up questions you may get

  1. What is the dominant resource at scale? Short answer: state-space size and whether the transition model is known — dynamic programming's O(|S|²|A|) cost and its memory footprint dominate before raw compute does.
  2. What assumption makes the mechanism work? The Markov property (next state depends only on current state and action) and γ < 1 giving a contraction mapping that guarantees convergence to a unique fixed point.
  3. Where does it fail first? When the chosen state representation is not actually Markov, or when the state/action space is too large to enumerate — both invalidate exact dynamic programming even though the MDP formalism itself remains valid.
  4. What is the simplest production baseline? Define the environment through an explicit, versioned interface (Gym/Gymnasium-style reset/step), validate the Markov assumption on real logged trajectories, and only then choose between dynamic programming (small, known model) or a learning-based method.
  5. What alternative would you choose? Compare exact dynamic programming, sample-based value learning, and policy-gradient methods against state-space size, model availability, and action-space continuity rather than declaring one universally superior.

15. What to remember

The Core Mental Model

MDPs & RL Foundations is best understood as the formal language that turns "make a good decision now, given that the future is uncertain and depends on this decision" into a recursive, computable equation — everything from tabular Q-learning to PPO is a different strategy for approximately solving the same Bellman equation.

The Production Insight

At scale, the main mechanism is only one component of the system. Whether the Markov property genuinely holds for your chosen state representation, and whether the state space is small enough to enumerate, determine which entire family of algorithms (exact dynamic programming vs. sample-based learning) is even available to you.

The Staff+/Research Insight

Every RL algorithm in this book's remaining chapters is best read as answering one question: how do you approximate the Bellman equation's fixed point when you cannot enumerate the state space and/or do not know the transition model? Recognizing which approximation a given method makes — sampling instead of exact expectation, a learned function instead of a table, bootstrapped instead of Monte Carlo returns — is more useful than memorizing each algorithm's name.

Bellman · 1957

What it demonstratedIntroduced the principle of optimality — that an optimal policy has the property that, whatever the initial decision, the remaining decisions must be optimal with respect to the state resulting from that decision — which is the mathematical origin of the Bellman equation.

How practitioners use itIt justifies why recursive backward induction (value iteration, and later TD learning) is a correct way to compute optimal behavior instead of an ad hoc heuristic.

Production Case Study — Standardizing the MDP interface with OpenAI Gym

System: OpenAI Gym / Gymnasium environment APIEvidence: public paper / documented systemScope: interface standardization for RL research and production

Binding constraint

Before a common interface existed, every RL codebase defined its own bespoke notion of state, action, reward, and episode termination. This made algorithms and environments non-interchangeable and results hard to reproduce.

Design intervention

Standardize every environment behind a minimal `reset()` / `step(action)` contract that returns an observation, reward, done flag, and info dict. This makes the MDP tuple (S, A, P, R) an explicit, swappable software boundary rather than an implicit assumption baked into each research codebase.
