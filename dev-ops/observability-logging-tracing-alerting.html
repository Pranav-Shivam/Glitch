Observability: Logging, Tracing & Alerting

A production-and-research treatment organized for engineers who already understand ML, neural networks, and Transformer fundamentals. Each chapter is deliberately split into two reading pages: mechanism first, then systems, failure modes, and research reasoning.

In plain English

Observability is building a system so that when something goes wrong in production, an engineer can answer "what, where, and why" from the telemetry it already emits, instead of guessing or reproducing the bug locally.

PAGE 1 · MECHANISM, MENTAL MODEL, THEORY

1. Quick explanation

Instrumenting a system with logs, metrics, and traces — the three pillars — and then defining service-level objectives and alerts on top of them so that failures are detected by their symptoms before a human notices the underlying cause.

2. What problem does this solve?

Problem. A distributed system made of many independently deployed services fails in combinations that no single service's local logs can explain, and a human cannot manually watch every dashboard for every service continuously.

Problem → constraint → design: distributed failures cross service boundaries and can't be diagnosed from one service's logs alone → signal-to-noise and correlation across services → emit structured logs, metrics, and distributed traces correlated by a shared request/trace ID, then alert on SLIs against an SLO, not on raw infra metrics

Why engineers care: removing the original bottleneck (no visibility) transfers pressure to alert fatigue and cardinality/cost of the telemetry itself. A useful implementation therefore instruments what a user actually experiences (symptom-based alerting) rather than paging on every internal metric that moves.

A useful way to think about it: logs are the "what happened" narrative for one instance, metrics are the "how much/how often" aggregate across the fleet, and traces are the "which path did this specific request take" — each pillar answers a question the other two cannot answer efficiently.

Where this analogy stops helping: the three pillars are not independent in a mature system — a good observability stack correlates them (exemplars linking a metric spike to the traces that caused it, trace IDs embedded in log lines) rather than treating them as three separate tools you tab between.

4. What is happening under the hood

  1. Represent the state. Every request is tagged with a trace ID at ingress (via a W3C Trace Context header) that propagates through every downstream call, and every log line is emitted as structured (JSON) key-value data rather than free text, carrying that same trace ID.
  2. Execute the topic-specific transformation. Metrics are scraped or pushed (Prometheus pull model, or OpenTelemetry's push-based OTLP) and aggregated into time series; traces are collected as spans (one per unit of work) and assembled into a trace by a collector (OpenTelemetry Collector, Jaeger, Tempo); logs are shipped to a log aggregator (Loki, Elasticsearch, CloudWatch Logs) indexed for correlation with the trace ID.
  3. Apply constraints and allocate resources. High-cardinality dimensions (user ID, request ID) are kept out of metric labels (they belong in traces/logs instead) to avoid cardinality explosion in the metrics backend; traces are sampled (head-based or tail-based) because capturing 100% of spans at scale is often cost-prohibitive.
  4. Preserve the right state. Dashboards (Grafana) visualize the metrics; alerting rules (Prometheus Alertmanager, or an SLO-burn-rate alert) fire against SLIs derived from those metrics, not against raw counters directly.
  5. Validate the failure boundary. The primary failure pressure is an alert that pages on a cause-level signal (CPU at 80%) instead of a symptom-level signal (user-facing error rate or latency breaching the SLO), producing pages that do not correspond to actual user harm.
error_budget = 1 − SLO; burn_rate = (observed_error_rate / (1 − SLO)); page when burn_rate is high enough to exhaust the budget before the window resets

The resource that usually becomes the problem: telemetry cardinality and ingestion/storage cost, and the on-call engineer's attention budget under alert fatigue.

6. How it scales

At a high level, as the workload grows: the number of services, request volume, and metric label cardinality all grow, and each multiplies telemetry ingestion cost. The practical bottleneck is cardinality and ingestion/storage cost of metrics and traces, not the alerting logic itself, which may dominate before the alerting rules become computationally expensive.

T_request = T_queue + T_compute + T_memory + T_network + T_validation

The practical question is: at what request volume and label cardinality does an unsampled, unbounded telemetry pipeline become the single most expensive line item in the infrastructure bill, forcing sampling, aggregation, or cardinality limits? That threshold is more actionable than a single big-O label.

7. How it works in production

OFFLINE / ASYNC

    ↓

    ↓
Review error-budget burn, tune thresholds

ONLINE / CRITICAL PATH

Request
    ↓
Ingress: assign trace ID, propagate context
    ↓
Instrumented service emits logs+metrics+spans
    ↓
Collector aggregates; SLO burn-rate alert evaluates
    ↓
Page on-call + dashboard for triage

A practical production starting point: instrument with OpenTelemetry (vendor-neutral SDK for logs, metrics, and traces) so you are not locked into one backend; export metrics to Prometheus and visualize in Grafana; define SLIs (request success rate, latency percentile) per user-facing service, set an SLO (e.g., 99.9% of requests under 300ms), and alert on multi-window, multi-burn-rate error-budget consumption rather than on raw CPU/memory thresholds.

Keep durable state behind explicit ownership boundaries; make workers independently replaceable; version caches and artifacts; isolate slow or failure-prone dependencies behind queues, deadlines, and budgets.

Use the simpler approach when: you run a single service or a small team without cross-service failure modes yet. Use the more production-ready approach when: multiple services interact and user-facing SLOs need enforcement. Choose advanced optimization when: a measured incident-response time problem (long mean-time-to-diagnose) justifies full distributed tracing with tail-based sampling. Avoid both naive simplification and premature optimization when: you have not yet defined what SLI actually reflects user-facing health for your service.

10. Common misunderstandings

Misconception: "More logging and more dashboards automatically improve observability."
Reality: observability is constrained by whether telemetry is structured, correlated, and mapped to a symptom-based SLI, so additional unstructured logs or unused dashboards add cost and noise without improving mean-time-to-diagnose.
Why people believe it: "we have logs for that" feels like coverage until the actual incident requires correlating three services' logs by hand.

Misconception: "100% CPU/memory alerting coverage means the system is well-monitored."
Reality: infra-level (cause) alerts frequently do not correlate with user-facing harm, and the SRE practice of symptom-based alerting exists specifically because cause-based alerting produces both false pages and missed real incidents.
Why people believe it: infra metrics are easy to collect and threshold, so they get treated as a proxy for reliability even when the correlation to user impact is weak.

Misconception: "Distributed tracing is a nice-to-have once logs and metrics exist."
Reality: in a microservices topology, logs and metrics alone cannot answer "which downstream call caused this request's latency", and adding tracing after an incident is too late to have captured the failing trace.
Why people believe it: tracing has a steeper initial instrumentation cost than logging, so teams defer it until a painful multi-day root-cause investigation makes the gap obvious.

11. The point where this starts to hurt

The system appears observable because dashboards are green and logs are flowing, but the real bottleneck is whether the alerting logic maps to actual user-facing harm and whether trace context survives every async hop. Capacity planning must therefore model alert-to-harm correlation and trace-context propagation coverage, not only dashboard existence.

12. How to explain it in an interview (30–45 seconds)

"Observability is instrumenting a system with the three pillars — logs, metrics, and traces — and then defining SLIs, SLOs, and error budgets on top so failures are detected by their user-facing symptoms rather than by an engineer noticing something looks off. It exists because a distributed system's failures cross service boundaries in ways a single service's local logs can't explain. Operationally, the core pipeline is: propagate a trace ID from ingress through every hop, emit structured logs and metrics tagged with that ID, aggregate via a collector like OpenTelemetry, and alert on SLO burn-rate rather than raw infra thresholds. I think of it as moving the detection surface from 'is the machine healthy' to 'is the user experience healthy', because those are frequently different questions. The main trade-off is telemetry cardinality and cost versus diagnostic depth — full tracing and high-cardinality metrics are powerful but expensive and require sampling decisions. In production I'd measure alert-to-incident correlation and mean-time-to-diagnose, because a system with beautiful dashboards and a noisy on-call rotation has an alerting problem, not an observability solution."

13. Follow-up questions you may get

  1. What is the dominant resource at scale? Short answer: metric/trace cardinality and ingestion cost, plus on-call attention budget under alert volume.
  2. What assumption makes the mechanism work? Deep explanation: that trace context propagates consistently across every synchronous and asynchronous hop; a single un-instrumented queue or worker breaks the causal chain silently.
  3. Where does it fail first? Trade-off: symptom-based SLO alerts can miss a slow-burning internal degradation that hasn't yet crossed the user-facing threshold, versus cause-based alerts that fire too early and too often.
  4. What is the simplest production baseline? Start with structured logging, a small set of low-cardinality metrics in Prometheus/Grafana, and one or two SLO-based burn-rate alerts per user-facing service, before adding full distributed tracing.
  5. What alternative would you choose? Compare head-based sampling (simpler, cheaper, may miss rare errors) against tail-based sampling (captures all errors/slow traces, requires buffering infrastructure) based on error rarity and budget.

15. What to remember

The Core Mental Model

Observability is best understood as building the causal chain — logs for narrative, metrics for aggregate trend, traces for cross-service path — and then defining alerts as a function of user-facing SLIs against an SLO, so a page always corresponds to measurable harm.

The Production Insight

At scale, the main mechanism is only one component of the system. Telemetry cardinality, sampling strategy, and alert-to-harm correlation determine whether the observability stack shortens incident diagnosis or just adds another set of dashboards nobody trusts.

The Staff+/Research Insight

The deeper principle is that observability is a feedback-control problem: the alerting system is only as good as the fidelity and latency of the signal it measures, so investing in correlated, low-latency, symptom-level telemetry pays off more than investing in dashboard breadth.

Production Case Study — Migrating from cause-based paging to SLO burn-rate alerting

System: Prometheus + Alertmanager + OpenTelemetry tracingEvidence: public documentation / documented SRE patternScope: alerting architecture

Binding constraint

An on-call rotation was paging on raw infrastructure thresholds (CPU > 80%, disk > 90%) across dozens of services, producing frequent pages that did not correlate with any user-facing degradation, which trained engineers to snooze pages — including, eventually, real ones.

Design intervention

The team defined a small number of SLIs per user-facing service (request success rate, p99 latency), set an SLO for each, and replaced infra-threshold alerts with multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts that page only when the error budget is being consumed fast enough to threaten the SLO within its compliance window, cross-referencing OpenTelemetry traces for the specific requests driving the burn.

cause-based infra thresholds → high page volume, low signal → SLI/SLO definition + burn-rate alerting → pages correspond to measurable user harm
SymptomRoot mechanismWhat to measure
Capacity, latency, quality, or reliability degrades under scale.Alerts were defined on infrastructure causes rather than user-facing symptoms, decoupling page volume from actual harm.Page-to-incident correlation rate, error-budget burn rate, and mean-time-to-diagnose after the alerting change.
Generalizable engineering lesson: An alert's value is measured by how often it corresponds to real user harm, not by how early it fires on an infrastructure metric.
